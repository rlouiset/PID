**W1 — Why the distinction matters and which limitation of standard PID it addresses.**

Standard PID returns a single redundancy atom, which conflates two situations with different meanings. In the AND/OR gates, the inputs are independent ($I(X_1;X_2)=0$), yet redundancy is non-zero: both inputs inform $Y$ in overlapping ways only because of the prediction rule. In AV-MNIST, by contrast, spoken and written digits encode the same concept, and redundancy arises from information the modalities genuinely share. This limitation is well recognized: Harder et al. (2013) introduced the terms *source* and *mechanistic* redundancy, Pica et al. (2017) formalized a related split into source and non-source redundancy, and Ince (2017b) showed it can be quantified via a partial entropy decomposition. These approaches, however, are restricted to low-dimensional discrete variables, and no estimator separates the two components in high-dimensional multimodal data, let alone per sample. Our supervised Gács–Körner formulation addresses this gap: source redundancy is the target information carried by a representation recoverable from both modalities, $f_1(X_1) \approx f_2(X_2) = Z^R$, with $R_{\text{src}} = I(Z^R;Y)$ (App. D), and mechanistic redundancy is the remainder.

The split matters because the two components support different conclusions: source redundancy indicates that the modalities measure a common underlying factor, whereas mechanistic redundancy indicates that independent factors lead to the same prediction, a notion closely related to degeneracy in biological systems (i.e., structurally different elements performing the same biological function; Tononi et al., 1999). Moreover, source redundancy comes with a shared representation $Z^R$, which makes it possible to localize the shared information. In Huntington's disease, the regions whose predictive importance is retained under speech alignment are the striatal motor regions (putamen and caudate), as expected: their degeneration drives dysarthria and contributes to the decline in lexical fluency. A permutation control reduces source redundancy to 0%. This illustrates the potential of the source/mechanistic split for biological interpretation, beyond what a single redundancy atom provides. We have added a paragraph making this motivation explicit in the introduction.




**Q1 — Can mechanistic redundancy be estimated directly?** Yes. A natural direct estimator is the redundancy that remains after conditioning on the source-redundant representation, $\tilde r_{\text{mech}} = \min\big(i(x_1;y \mid z^R),\, i(x_2;y \mid z^R)\big)$ (with the sign criterion of Eq. 3), computed from predictors $p(y \mid x_m, z^R)$. When the modalities share no information (logic gates), $z^R$ is uninformative and $\tilde R_{\text{mech}} = R$; when all redundancy is shared (AV-MNIST), conditioning on $z^R$ removes it and $\tilde R_{\text{mech}} \approx 0$. Because the pointwise minimum is not additive, $\tilde R_{\text{mech}}$ need not equal $R - R_{\text{src}}$ exactly, but the two provide independent estimates that should agree. We describe this estimator in Appendix and leave its systematic evaluation to future work.

**Q1 — Two-bit copy: how should $R_{\text{mech}}$ be interpreted?**

We thank the reviewer for this important example. The two-bit copy is a well-known contested case in the PID literature, with no consensus decomposition. Measures satisfying the identity property (Harder et al., 2013; Bertschinger et al., 2014) assign 1 bit of unique information to each source, relying on the fact that $Y=(X_1,X_2)$ has two components, each copied from one source. $I_{\min}$ (Williams & Beer, 2010), which our estimator follows, instead quantifies redundancy at the level of target *outcomes*: for every outcome $y$, each source rules out exactly half of the four classes, so both provide the same specific information about every outcome (redundancy), while identifying the outcome requires observing both (synergy). This reading is natural when $Y$ is a categorical label with no internal structure, as in classification. Both decompositions are consistent with the vanishing co-information $I(X_1;X_2;Y)=0$, and the identity property is known to be incompatible with the Williams–Beer axioms and non-negativity beyond two sources (Rauh et al., 2014).

Empirically, our estimator recovers the $I_{\min}$ decomposition: $R = [0.672]$ and $S = [0.679]$ nats (vs. $\log 2 = 0.693$), with $U_1, U_2 \approx [0.02]$ (new App. [X]).

Crucially, **$R_{\text{source}}$ does not depend on this convention.** Since $X_1 \perp X_2$, no information is shared between the modalities, and SupGK correctly returns $R_{\text{source}} = [0.000]$ nats (λ = 10; the SupGK representations carry no information about $Y$). The redundancy is therefore attributed entirely to $R_{\text{mech}}$.

This clarifies how $R_{\text{mech}}$ should be interpreted: it is the *residual* predictive redundancy not explained by information shared between the modalities, and its meaning follows the convention of the base redundancy measure. Under $I_{\min}$, it includes redundancy in the "same amount of information about each outcome" sense, as in this example. Because the source/mechanistic split is modular, SupGK can be combined with an identity-satisfying measure, in which case $R = R_{\text{mech}} = 0$ here. We have revised Sec. 3 to define $R_{\text{mech}}$ as this residual and to state its dependence on the base measure explicitly.

**Q2 — Partial-source setting.** We thank the reviewer for this sharp example. The reviewer is right that, for pairs differing only in $A$, the cross-modal alignment and different-class separation terms of Eq. 7 pull in opposite directions, since $X_2 = C$ cannot distinguish them. We address it theoretically and empirically.

*Population optimum.* Under the hard Gács–Körner constraint, the optimum is $Z^R = f(C)$, which gives $R_{\text{source}} = I(C;Y) = \log 2$, the correct value. The separation term then only adds a constant penalty for pairs that differ in $A$ alone.

*The conflict cannot inflate the estimate.* Pointwise source redundancy is the minimum over modalities (Eq. 8), so $r_{\text{source}} \le i(z_2^R; y)$. Since $z_2^R$ is a function of $X_2 = C$ only, its information about $Y$ is at most $I(C;Y) = \log 2$ on average, whatever happens to $z_1^R$. The only possible effect of the conflict is therefore an *under*estimation.

*Empirically.* We ran exactly this example (new App. [X]; values in nats, mean over [3] seeds). The ground truth is $R = R_{\text{source}} = \log 2 = 0.693$, $U_1 = 0.693$, $U_2 = S = R_{\text{mech}} = 0$. Our estimator recovers $R = [0.691]$, $U_1 = [0.686]$, $U_2 = [0.000]$, $S = [0.009]$. SupGK gives $R_{\text{source}} = [0.58\text{–}0.62]$ across $\lambda$, i.e. it never exceeds the true value and underestimates it by [10–15]%, the cost of the tension identified by the reviewer. As a result, a small part of the redundancy ($[0.08\text{–}0.11]$ nats) is attributed to $R_{\text{mech}}$ instead of $R_{\text{source}}$. We now discuss this conservative bias in Sec. 3.2 and note that partial-source settings are where SupGK is least accurate.
